01. Overview

Sense Clip is designed for deaf and hard-of-hearing wearers who can miss the one-off sounds that help people stay oriented: a knock, a name being called, a car horn, an alarm, or a siren.

The device listens through its own microphone, classifies the type of sound locally, then plays a matching vibration rhythm and LED colour. It performs sound-event recognition with haptic encoding—it does not transcribe speech, translate language, or reveal what anyone said.

DESIGN PRINCIPLE

No part of the interface depends on hearing. Every action receives a visible LED acknowledgement, and the important actions also receive a felt response from the motor.

02. How it works

The complete path—from sound in the room to a pattern on the wrist—runs locally on the microcontroller. No phone, network connection, or cloud service is part of the intended operation.

  1. STEP 01ListenThe digital microphone continuously samples the acoustic environment at 16 kHz.
  2. STEP 02RecognizeFirmware segments an event and compares its level, duration, pitch, texture, and timing.
  3. STEP 03SignalThe matching rhythm is queued; the motor vibrates and the LED shows the same class colour.

THE SIGNAL PIPELINE

From audio frame to haptic alert

  1. Capture

    Digital I²S audio enters the microcontroller at 16 kHz, avoiding an analogue microphone trace beside the motor drive line.

  2. Frame

    Every 10 ms, the firmware applies a Hann window to 256 samples and runs a 256-point FFT.

  3. Track the room

    An adaptive noise floor follows changing ambience. An event opens at 10 dB above the floor and closes after 320 ms below floor + 5 dB.

  4. Extract features

    Level, duration, spectral peak, pitch movement, flatness, onset count, and onset spacing describe the sound in measurable terms.

  5. Classify

    A hand-written rule set checks siren, horn, alarm, knock, and call patterns in priority order; an unusually loud class catches unmatched events.

  6. Schedule the haptic

    Priority and per-class cooldown decide whether the new alert interrupts, waits, or is dropped. Rhythm and LED colour always agree.

WHY RHYTHM

One motor, a vocabulary in time

REJECTED

Intensity

“Strong” is not a stable signal: perceived amplitude changes with strap tension, wrist size, and arm position.

REJECTED

Location

Multiple motors add cost, wiring, and volume, while the wrist has relatively coarse two-point discrimination.

SELECTED

Rhythm

Pulse count and pulse length are easier to distinguish. One motor is enough, and the vocabulary remains configurable in software.

03. Sound-to-vibration vocabulary

Each category has a unique pulse structure, colour, urgency, and cooldown. Sirens and horns run at full motor duty; other alerts use 85%.

PRIORITY

Urgent events cut through

A higher-numbered event interrupts the pattern already playing. A siren can interrupt a doorbell; a doorbell cannot interrupt a siren.

COOLDOWN

Repeated sounds stay readable

Each class has its own cooldown, preventing a doorbell pressed six times from becoming one continuous and confusing vibration.

CONFIGURABLE

Patterns live in software

Pulse timing, colour, priority, repeats, and cooldown can be changed over USB serial without rebuilding the firmware.

LEARNING MODE

Practice before it matters

Holding the button for 1.5 seconds cycles through every class twice, pairing each vibration with its LED colour.

04. Physical construction

Rev B is a six-part printed enclosure built to stay reopenable, protect the LiPo cell, carry a standard strap, and print without support.

  1. Caseback floor

    A 1.2 mm solid wrist-facing layer is the only material between the vibration motor and the wearer’s skin.

  2. Cell + motor pockets

    A protected 502030 LiPo sits in its own bay. A 10 × 2.7 mm coin motor sits beside it on a pad raised 0.4 mm toward the skin.

  3. Air gap

    A verified 0.4 mm gap remains above the cell, preventing the enclosure from compressing it when closed.

  4. Carrier board

    A 47 × 28 mm perfboard rests on two moulded ribs at 7.2–8.8 mm above the wrist face.

  5. Electronics headroom

    The MCU, microphone, tactile switch, and RGB LED occupy 4.6 mm above the carrier board.

  6. Top wall

    The 2.0 mm cover carries the microphone port, flexible TPU button pad, and clear PETG light pipe.

ACOUSTIC PATH

Sealed path to the microphone

A 1.8 mm top port, recessed 5 mm mesh disc, 4.0 mm moulded bore, and compressed silicone ring guide sound onto the bottom-ported microphone face.

SERVICEABILITY

Four screws from the wrist side

Four M2 screws pass through caseback counterbores into bosses on the main shell, allowing the case to be reopened with an ordinary screwdriver.

SUPPORT-FREE PRINT

Flat faces become build plates

The main case prints top-face down and the caseback prints wrist-face down. Internal bosses, ribs, duct, and lugs grow upward without unsupported geometry.

05. Preventing false alerts

The hardest engineering problem is that a microphone and vibration motor share one small enclosure. If the wristband classified its own motor noise, it could create a false alert loop.

01 / DISTANCE

Opposite ends of the case

The microphone and motor are 29.9 mm apart and diagonally opposed in plan view.

02 / ISOLATION

No direct rigid path

The motor bonds to the caseback while the microphone rides on a 1.0 mm silicone pad on the rib-supported board.

03 / FIRMWARE

Classifier blanking

Recognition pauses from 30 ms before the first haptic pulse until 120 ms after the last. Any event already open is abandoned rather than contaminated by motor noise.

TRADEOFF

Deaf while buzzing

The device cannot recognize new sounds during an alert. A three-repeat siren pattern leaves it unable to listen for roughly seven seconds.

06. Electronics, controls, and power

The planned prototype uses off-the-shelf development modules on perfboard, so a working unit can be assembled without a custom PCB.

Seeed Studio XIAO ESP32-C3, 21 × 17.5 mm, with USB-C and LiPo charging on board.
MICROPHONE

MOTOR

POWER REALITY

Battery life is modelled, not measured. With continuous I²S capture and FFT processing on the ESP32-C3, the likely region is 25–40 mA: an honest charge-every-night prototype. A lower-power MCU is a future product decision.

07. Privacy by operation

The intended firmware uses the microphone as a sensor, not a recorder. Processing remains on the device.

01

Audio stays in RAM. Frames are analysed in a ring buffer and continuously overwritten; audio is never written to flash.

02

Events are not archived. A recognized class label is used immediately to schedule an alert, then discarded.

03

Radios stay off. Wi-Fi and Bluetooth are disabled at startup; there is no pairing or network connection.

04

USB is the only output. Human-readable diagnostic logs leave the device only while a cable is connected.

These are properties of the planned firmware, not guarantees enforced by hardware. Reflashing different firmware could change them, and using any always-listening device around other people still carries a social responsibility.

KNOWN LIMITATION 01

A two-tone door chime currently falls through to the “unusually loud” pattern. The wearer is alerted, but with the wrong class.

KNOWN LIMITATION 02

Speech is not distinguished from someone calling the wearer, so normal conversation can trigger the call class.

KNOWN LIMITATION 03

Simultaneous events produce one alert; the higher-priority class wins.

NEXT TESTS / OPEN QUESTIONS

  1. T-01Battery charge current

    Measure the board’s charge current before the first cell is connected.

  2. T-08Self-vibration rejection

    Confirm that a real enclosure does not classify its own motor as a new event.

PROTOTYPE BOUNDARY

No physical unit or user study exists yet, and no real-world recognition accuracy has been measured. Sense Clip is not a certified alarm or medical device, has no water-resistance rating, and is not a replacement for a smoke alarm, fire alarm, CO detector, or medical alert device.
